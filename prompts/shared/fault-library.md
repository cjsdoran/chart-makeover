# Fault-and-fix library

When the EM finds one of these faults, it proposes the matching fix. Each is a pattern from the class examples, written in general terms so it applies to any chart.

| Fault | Why it fails | Fix the EM proposes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absolute values over time in a growing market | Market growth lifts every line; the outlier looks like one riser among many | Index each entity to the market average each period (1.0 = average) |
| Share of the entity's own total (e.g. % of own revenue) | The denominator moves for unrelated reasons | Compare with the market or peers, not with the entity's own total |
| Many coloured lines under a topic title | No emphasis; the reader hunts for the point | Subject in one colour, the rest grey; a lead with a number |
| One period used to measure a long-run driver | Noisy, and can reverse cause and effect | A multi-period average, with the window tested against alternatives |
| A hand-picked handful of peers | Too few to define normal | All entities with data, plus a trend line |
| Dual axis | Two scales invite false crossings | One X-Y plot, points joined in time order (rule C7) |
| Two levels plotted when the lead is about the gap between them | The reader has to subtract in their head | Add the gap as its own line, data labels rounded to the nearest 10; mark start, end and % change |
| A proxy measure | It does not measure what the lead claims | Plot the true measure (salary uplift; total cost including forgone salary) |
| Unconnected dots for a relationship over time | The sequence is hard to follow | Connect them in time order; label start, turning point and end |
| Turning point not marked | The proof of the lead is left for the reader to find | Annotate the turning point; draw and label slope lines before and after |
| Series starting at different points | Unfair comparison | Align them to a common start ("day zero") |
| Wide range on a linear axis | Small values vanish; growth rates are hard to compare | Log scale, labelled; constant growth becomes a straight line |
| A colour per series when the lead is about groups | The reader has to group them mentally | One colour per group, each group labelled once |
| Too few points to show the pattern | The lead is not proven | Extend the series over the same span for every entity |
| A dense table of unrounded numbers | The reader hunts for the key figures | Round, cut columns that are not needed, highlight the key column, put the lead in the title |
| Numbers shown both as labels and on an axis | Duplication | Keep the data labels and drop the axis, or the reverse |
| The lead's number buried in an axis or a small label | The reader has to search for the proof | Show the number in the emphasis colour, bold and larger, beside the mark it describes |
| A number in the lead that the chart contradicts (wrong sum, wrong measure) | The reader stops trusting the page | Correct the number, show it on the chart, and highlight it in colour or size |
| A pasted Excel table as the chart | No visual focus; the client needs five minutes to work it out | Hand it back for a lead; move the table to the appendix for Q&A; chart only what proves the lead |
| A pie chart | Slices are hard to compare; 3D looks unprofessional | A sorted bar, or the measure the decision needs; detailed numbers in an appendix table |
| Growth shown only as lines or columns | Nobody can eyeball a growth rate | CAGR % per series at the right end, or in the chart title with the period |
| Stacked columns with an axis, gridlines and a legend | Clutter; the reader matches colours to a key | Drop the axis, gridlines and legend; label each segment left of the first column; round the labels; add totals above and CAGR % per segment on the right; consider ranked CAGR bars beside it |
| A lead that jumps to an action from one chart | The action needs more evidence (e.g. a market-entry call also needs profitability and competitive position) | State the finding the chart supports; name the further analysis the action needs |
| A standard framework with its axes swapped | Readers misread a familiar layout | Keep the convention (GE matrix: attractiveness vertical); label the dividing lines |
| Artificial numbers (1–5 scores) in the lead | False precision | Say the finding in words, or use a real number |
